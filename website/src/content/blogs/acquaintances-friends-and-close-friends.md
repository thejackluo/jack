> A framework for thinking about people

This is not a universal law of human relationships. It is just my current way of organizing how I think about people in my life. Your circumstances might be completely different. Treat this as a framework to think about, push back on, or discuss with someone, not as something everyone has to follow.

When I zoom out and look at my life, almost everyone around me falls into three categories:

- Acquaintances
- Friends
- Close friends

The labels themselves are common, but the way you treat each category, and how much time you feed into each, has a huge impact on how your life feels. That is what I want to unpack.

---

## Acquaintances: The surface network that actually matters

Acquaintances are the easiest to define.

These are people I can say hi to, make small talk with, maybe do business with, and generally interact with in a light way. We recognize each other, we know a bit about what the other person does, and there is some level of familiarity, but not a lot of emotional depth.

Most business relationships live here. They are transactional and value based. We collaborate, exchange favors, or help each other once in a while. That can sound cold, but I actually think acquaintances are underrated.

If you care about financial freedom or having more opportunities in life, acquaintances are usually the ones who bring most of your chances. Weak ties are powerful. The random person you met once who later forwards you a job posting. The person from a club who remembers that you do AI and sends you a lead. The investor you had one decent call with who introduces you to someone else. That kind of thing.

Because of that, I think it is worth being deliberate here. The best way I know to manage acquaintances is to treat them with a kind of human CRM mindset. Not because I want to turn people into entries in a database, but because I do not trust my memory. If I write down who I met, where I met them, what they care about, and what they are working on, I can actually be considerate later when I see an update about them or find something useful to send.

In Chinese culture there is the idea of guanxi, a web of relationships and mutual obligations built over time. Acquaintances are the outer layer of that web. You do small favors, you stay loosely in touch, you keep the goodwill alive. Over years, that compounds.

The nice part is that you do not need heavy emotional attachment for this. These relationships are mostly value based. If something does not work out, you move on. There are many other people. That lack of pressure actually makes it easier to talk to them.

So my rough take is that you should absolutely spend a decent amount of time tending to acquaintances. Not obsessively, but consistently. They give you options and surface area in life that you cannot get alone.

## Friends: The comfortable middle that can quietly eat your time

“Friend” is the most slippery category.

It is obvious who is just an acquaintance. It is obvious who is a real close friend. The middle zone is where everything gets blurry.

For me, a friend is someone I can grab lunch with, someone I can do small talk with and occasionally go deeper, and someone I enjoy being around in the context of an activity. The important part is that the relationship is usually attached to something else.

Think bars, restaurants, club meetings, parties, game nights, events. A friend is the person you bring so you are not going alone. They make the experience more social and more fun. You can talk about classes, work, gossip, random thoughts. It is enjoyable.

But there is a limit. If a friend suggested something I really do not care about, like going to an American Girl store, I probably would not go unless I cared about them much more than I admit, or the activity secretly interests me. The person alone is usually not enough to drag me into something I would never do otherwise. With close friends, that starts to flip.

This middle category is also where most people pour a lot of time, especially at the start of college or when moving to a new city. The first instinct is to meet as many people as possible and do as many things as possible. I do not think that is wrong. You need volume to find the few people who eventually become close friends.

The problem is what happens later. If you have been interacting with someone for a long time and things are not really progressing, if you are not becoming closer or talking about deeper things, and you are just looping the same surface level hangouts, then I think it is better to be honest and make a choice.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*3G4C8b-gByJDu1_8WjLAXg@2x.jpeg)

Either you demote them to acquaintance in your own mind, or you actively see if there is potential to upgrade them to close friend. What you do not want is permanent “medium.” You keep investing time because you do not want to lose them, but you are not getting much long term fulfillment out of it. It is like eating a bag of chips. You get short term happiness. If you keep doing that as your main diet, you feel worse over time.

A lot of friend tier interactions are like that. Parties, superficial club stuff, going out just for the sake of it. You get dopamine. You do not always get deep emotional nourishment.

My current opinion is that you should probably spend the least time in this category. Not zero, just the least. It has the worst tradeoff. It is not as flexible and low pressure as acquaintances, and it is not as fulfilling as close friends, yet it still consumes a lot of time. On top of that you get the sunk cost effect. Once you have invested enough into a friendship, you do not want to “break the ice bridge” you built, so you keep walking back and forth even when the other side is not that meaningful for you anymore.

## Close friends: The compounding habit

Close friends feel different.

These are the people I would go out of my way to do things with. I would travel with them. I would reschedule my day to see them. I would help them with favors that genuinely inconvenience me, simply because I care.

I do that because the relationship and the benefits I get from the interaction are much deeper than with ordinary friends. If I had to put a number on it, maybe it feels three or four times more meaningful. I walk away from these interactions feeling nourished, not just entertained.

You cannot have too many close friends, not because you do not want more, but because you are limited. There is only so much time and emotional energy you can give. That scarcity is part of what makes the category meaningful. If you call ten or fifteen people your close friend, the phrase stops meaning anything.

It is also hard to build close friendships. You need both consistency and circumstances that line up. You might like someone a lot, but your schedules never match. You might have one deep conversation but live in different cities. You might click, but they are in a different life phase and have no bandwidth. It feels very similar to dating. There are many people you would enjoy being around, and many who would enjoy being around you, but the overlap where both people care and the timing works is rare.

Those are the people you protect.

I also think of close friendships as a compounding habit. Just like lifting or writing, relationships get better the more consistently you invest. The closer you are with someone, the more you can talk about serious things, the more you can try weird or “crazy” experiences together, and the more comfortable everything becomes. You filter less. You perform less. You can actually show your real thoughts.

At the end of the day a close friendship should be nourishing. You should walk away feeling happier, more grounded, and more understood. From a pure utility theory perspective, time spent here is some of the best “relationship time” you can invest. Beyond utility, it is also one of the most fulfilling things a person can do: choose a small number of people and go very deep.

### How cults and gangs hack the close friend wiring

There is also a darker side to all of this.

Cults and gangs both exploit the human hunger for close friends and belonging. They target people who are lonely, disconnected, or lacking a stable community. Then they rush to simulate that third category, the feeling of intense closeness.

They shower you with attention. They make you feel seen and welcomed. They give you language like “family” and “brothers.” Humans will disproportionately invest in what they believe are close relationships. Once you feel that, you are willing to sacrifice way more.

That emotional leverage can push people into donating large sums of money, cutting off previous support systems, and doing things that go against their own morals. In the worst cases they become completely indoctrinated and cut off from the rest of society.

Gangs work in a similar way. People who join often start from a place of loneliness, broken families, or lack of community. The gang offers validation, protection, and identity. Relationships live in the middle of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. If that layer is empty, almost any group that offers belonging starts to look attractive, even when the cost is very high.

So the same wiring that makes close friendships incredibly positive can also be used against you when you are desperate. That is another reason to be deliberate about who you let into that inner circle.

## How to treat each category

At this point the question is what to do with this framework in practice.

For acquaintances, I think the right attitude is systematic kindness. Use some kind of light CRM so you do not forget people. Note who you met, what they care about, and what they are doing. Check in once in a while. Send something relevant when you see it. You are not trying to fake intimacy. You are trying to respect the fact that you have crossed paths and might be useful to each other in the future. Over a long horizon these weak ties become a kind of economic and social safety net. They give you optionality.

For friends, you have to be more ruthless. At the beginning of a new phase in life, like starting college or moving, it makes sense to meet a lot of people and say yes to a lot of things. That is the exploration stage. But after a while you need to be honest with yourself about which friendships are going somewhere and which ones are basically short term entertainment. If a friendship has been stuck at the same shallow level for a long time, either gently downgrade it to acquaintance in your mind or actively test whether it can become deeper. What you want to avoid is drifting for years in the middle.

For close friends, I think you should unapologetically overinvest. Spend time. Rearrange things sometimes. Talk about real topics. Do meaningful or even slightly absurd things together. Check in when they are struggling. Support their ambitions. Let the relationship compound over time. It is one of the few places where being “inefficient” with your time is actually rational.

## Four common social builds

There are infinitely many ways to arrange your social life, but I keep noticing four common patterns. Think of them as different builds in a game. Different stats, different tradeoffs. This is not scientific, just patterns I keep seeing.

### Type 1: The community build

The first type is the person who orbits a community most of the time.

They are tied to some group, a hobby, a club, a dorm, a workplace, a religious community. Most of their social life comes bundled with that environment. Out of that they get a lot of acquaintances, a set of friends, and maybe one or two people who could become close friends.

This build is very generalist and very safe. You will not be isolated. You will have events to go to and people to talk to. Your life will look socially normal from the outside.

The downside is that depth does not happen automatically. It is much easier to stay at the “friend” level than to upgrade someone to close friend. Demoting someone to acquaintance is much easier mentally than promoting them. Promotion takes initiative and risk. You have to break out of the script of just seeing each other at group events.

If you are in this build, I would not tell you to leave your community. I would encourage you to look carefully at who you actually vibe with, who you respect, and who you would want as a close friend if things lined up. Then invest more in those people and slightly less in low value, short term fun that you already know will not matter in a year.

### Type 2: The loner build

The second type is the loner.

This is the person who does not really talk to anyone at all besides customer service. I think there are more people here than we admit. In Japan there is already a word, hikikomori, for people who retreat from society. I think similar things exist elsewhere, just not as clearly defined.

For people in this category, the first thing that matters is environment. Are you alone because your surroundings are genuinely bad for you, or because you are scared, or because you do not know where to start socially. Those are different cases.

In my head there are three stages here:

- Bad acquaintances and bad friends
- Being alone
- Good acquaintances and good friends

Being alone is not automatically the worst state. If the people around you are toxic and constantly pulling you into things that hurt you, then being alone is an upgrade. You get space to think, be independent, and decide what you want next.

So if you are in the loner build, step one is not to throw yourself into any group just to stop feeling lonely. Step one is to be honest about whether your current environment has any potential for good people at all. If it does, then you can start with small, low pressure attempts to meet better acquaintances and test new spaces. If it does not, then your loneliness might actually be a transitional phase while you figure out how to move somewhere better.

I see this build as an intermediate stage that can be useful. Long term, most people are better off with at least a few good acquaintances and a handful of close friends.

### Type 3: The over networked superconnector

Type three is the over networked person, the superconnector.

This is the person who seems to know everyone. They can introduce you to people. They belong to multiple different networks at once. They are inside several circles, several group chats, and several communities at the same time.

On the surface, a lot of people want to be like this. It looks powerful. It looks high status. There are real benefits. You can get more favors done. You have leverage. You can route opportunities and people.

What surprised me, after talking to some people like this, is how many of them feel insecure or even lonely underneath. They are spread across so many relationships that none of them goes very deep. They have many acquaintances and many friends, but few close friends. Each relationship gets a small investment and yields a small return. It is enough to be sustainable, but not enough to be meaningful.

Investing in communities and broad networks is like buying an index fund. It is safe, diversified, and will probably give you some returns. Investing in close friends is more like picking a stock you really believe in. If you are right, the upside is huge. Your life gets much better because of a few people. If you are wrong, you get hurt. It is a gamble, but I think it is a worthy gamble as long as you pursue what you believe is good.

Relationships are amplifiers. Good ones make you a better person. Bad ones push you in the opposite direction.

If you are over networked, I do not think you need more people. You need to rebalance. Keep the network if you like it, but consciously pull some time out of the “everyone” bucket and pour it into a few people who actually could be close friends. Say no a bit more often to random events, and yes a bit more often to slower, deeper time with a small core.

### Type 4: The close friend core

Type four is the person who has a small set of close friends and spends most free time with them.

If you are in this group, you have a few people you truly care about and prioritize. You see them regularly. You talk about real things. You might travel together, build things together, or just share life in a steady way.

Out of all the configurations, I think this is the best baseline, even though it is not perfect. Most of your relationship investment goes into deep connections that actually fulfill you. You are not completely lonely, and you are not shallowly spread across everyone. You have an amplifier. Your close friends make you feel more stable and more understood.

You could live a long life with this setup and feel quite fulfilled.

I still think there are ways to improve it. Just having close friends is not enough. You also want a ring of acquaintances for business and small things, and you want some friend level people and maybe a group to add variety. I see it as concentric circles. The close friends are the foundation. Around them are friends and friend groups. Around that, acquaintances.

If you are already in type four, your main job is maintenance and balance. Protect and deepen the core, keep acquaintances alive, and occasionally let new people move inward if it makes sense.

## The ideal configuration, for me

Again, what is ideal for me may not be ideal for you. But after thinking about all this, here is what I would personally aim for.

The ideal foundation is a set of close friends. That is the first concentric circle.

Ideally you have both some people in other cities and at least a few people who are physically close. Distance matters a lot more than we like to admit. Long distance friendships are possible, but they are harder to maintain. In my head I have this rough picture that if someone is twice as far away, the relationship naturally becomes something like one quarter as effective. That is obviously not a precise formula, but it captures the idea that distance kills spontaneity.

The same kind of intuition applies to communication. You might text one person twice as much as another, but feel four times closer to them. It is not exact, there are exceptions, and there are people you can be close to even without constant texting. Still, in general, proximity and frequency matter. You want people you can actually see. You want to be able to meet in person when it counts.

Online relationships are possible, and sometimes necessary, but I do not think they give the same type of satisfaction as in person ones. At least for me.

So the first ring is close friends you can talk to about anything, some near you and some far away. Ideally, at least one or two of them are in the same city so you can actually hang out without it becoming a logistical nightmare.

The second ring is friends. Even though I said earlier that you should not overspend time here, I still think it is useful to have a small group of people in this category. Some of them might eventually become close friends. Some might stay as friends, and that is fine.

One way to think about this is to intentionally form a small group around something. It could be a hacker house, a community of people who study together, a small project crew, or just a group that meets for a particular activity. Give yourself a reason to gather. Try a few times. If it works, then over time you will have a stable group that can meet here and there.

This is tricky, because people are busy and schedules do not align. A whole friend group meet up might only happen once every few months. That is still worth it. Consistency matters more than frequency. It could be a week every year, or a small recurring tradition. The point is to keep that group alive enough that it does not just fade out.

The outer ring is acquaintances. Here my advice is to be very generous with the people you meet. Be open. Talk to people. Add them to your mental or actual CRM. Check in once in a while. A lot of opportunity and randomness comes from this layer, and it does not cost that much to keep it going.

If you put this all together, the ideal configuration for me looks like a set of concentric circles: a solid core of close friends, a smaller ring of friends and friend groups, and a wide ring of acquaintances that you treat with basic respect and curiosity.

You do not have to copy this. You might be more introverted, more extroverted, more career focused, more family focused. The details will shift based on your values. But I think it is worth at least asking yourself where your current life fits in this picture, and whether the way you spend time with people matches what actually makes you feel alive.
